Little published criticism exists. What there is comes mostly from Tom Johnson, who writes on technical documentation.

His first objection was rigidity: four silos looked opinionated and arbitrary. He withdrew it after corresponding with Procida, on the ground that the four types come from reader need rather than from an imposed scheme. He still holds that the scheme may oversimplify, and that the divisions are not absolute in practice, while remaining useful as an abstraction.

His second point is about the future rather than the framework. If a chatbot becomes the main way a reader reaches documentation, the architecture of a documentation set matters less, because nobody navigates it. He also notes the inverse: the four patterns give a useful target when prompting a model to sort unstructured material.

The framework itself names the pressure point. A how-to guide is not always reducible to a procedure, because real problems fork and need judgement. Content that overlaps two categories, of which "getting started" mixing concepts with installation steps is the standard case, is the usual audit finding.
